Zero to
Fund One

Raising a venture fund is
simpler than you think.

Refoundable

As Head of Admissions at VC Lab, a venture fund accelerator in Silicon Valley, I talk to hundreds of aspiring managers a year about their fund plans. Over time, I've noticed clear patterns of failure and success, and I've distilled them here into ten mistakes to avoid and seven questions to answer before you start a fund. Get these right, and you'll simplify your plan and close your fund faster.

Mistakes to avoid:

  • Riding trends: It’s easy to string together a list of buzzwords and call it a strategy, but a label like “climate tech” or “AI” is a starting point, not a thesis. It doesn’t answer the real question LPs care about: what do you know about these deals that other investors don’t? Your thesis should be so specific and exclusive that only you could have written it.
  • Hand-waving value: Most investors like to think they’re smart money, yet few can say exactly how they add value. Start by tracking what you’ve already done for founders in your space. That scorecard shows founders how you’ll help them win, and proves your edge to LPs.
  • Partnering with résumés: Whenever a team is built like a consulting firm, with a specialist for every skill, that’s almost never a good sign. A fund, much like a startup, is a ten-year marriage, and divorce gets ugly. So choose people you enjoy working with, who will be as excited about the thesis, five years in, as you are. If you can’t find the right people, go solo and bring in venture partners to help with deals.
  • Asking too much: Raising $100M sounds impressive until you miss the mark, which can disappoint your LPs and kill your next fund. Fund size is an output, not a target: pick a small number you can hit, then beat it.
  • Playing for fees: When managers deliberate on how much to raise, too often, they do the fee math first, then size the fund to cover overhead, which is how fund targets balloon. However, the purpose of fund one is to get to fund two, not to pay for a corner office. Your budget should cover the basics and grow with the firm. Raise a new fund every two years, and soon your overhead will be spread across several funds.
  • Reserving for follow-ons: Investors assume they should reserve up to a third of their fund for follow-ons, except that doing so in fund one only stretches out the raise. You’re better off writing only first checks, then helping your LPs double down on winners through side vehicles.
  • Chasing big names: A college endowment or pension fund looks great on paper, but most are barred by charter from backing first funds, and the few that aren’t will tie you up in red tape that costs you deals. Best to treat fund one as a proof of concept: raise a small amount quickly from people who trust you, and start building your track record.
  • Selling to strangers: If you’re not comfortable raising from people you know, you shouldn’t start a fund. LP intros and public marketing are no shortcut, and broadcasting your raise can break securities rules. Your fund is an exclusive opportunity: remember that you’re the prize, and only people connected to you get access.
  • Hiding behind a deck: The best managers don’t lead with a deck because they know that it turns a conversation into a sales pitch, and nobody wants to be sold. Talk through your idea with people you trust and ask for their feedback. Save the deck for after they say yes. You’re not pitching, you’re enrolling.
  • Taking too long: It takes an average of 300 conversations to raise your first fund. You can’t directly control that number, only how fast you get through it. Starting part-time is fine, but once you see traction, go full-time until you close, even if that means taking leave from your job.

Deal flow questions:

  • Access: What network are you part of that most investors can’t join? Name a founder you met there before they raised.
  • Blind spot: What do most investors miss about the founders you want to back that will be obvious in five years?
  • Value: In the last year, how many founders came to you for help? What tangible win did you deliver: a hire, a customer, a round?

Fundraising questions:

  • Commitment: Are you and your partners equally obsessed with your space, enough to go full-time once momentum picks up?
  • Patrons: How many people you know have backed a startup or fund before? How many of them have seen you work up close?
  • First close: What’s the smallest fund you could close in three months with people you already know?
  • Sequence: What will fund one need to prove in two years for your LPs to back your next fund?
